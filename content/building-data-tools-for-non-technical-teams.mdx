A useful dashboard explains what its numbers mean, which period they cover and what is missing. People should not need technical training to understand it.

## Start with the reader's question

Choose the information around what someone needs to learn or decide. A person exploring a subject may need definitions. Someone checking a weekly report may need the latest figure and a comparison with last week.

My work on workforce data experiences has made me pay attention to the explanations and navigation around the figures. Finding the correct report is part of using it successfully.

Give each screen a clear purpose before adding more charts.

## Explain numbers where they appear

Use labels people recognise and define unfamiliar terms nearby. Show the reporting period, the group being counted and when the information was updated.

For example, “Customers” could mean everyone registered, people who bought something this month or currently active accounts. A short label such as “Customers who ordered in October” makes the meaning clearer.

An empty chart also needs an explanation. “No matching records” and “Data unavailable” describe different situations. Showing a zero for missing information can give the wrong impression.

## Make filters visible

Filters let people narrow a report, for example by location or date. Show which choices are currently applied and how to clear them.

If a filter changes what a total represents, update the nearby label too. Someone sharing a screenshot should be able to tell which period or group it describes.

Choose a useful starting view, but let readers change it without losing track of their selections.

## Offer another way to read the result

Colour should not be the only way to distinguish chart categories. Use labels, and provide a table or short written explanation where practical.

Check that people can operate the filters with a keyboard and read the content on a phone. Keep limitations near the affected figure, especially when information is incomplete or estimates are involved.

Before adding another chart, ask a person unfamiliar with the tool to explain what the existing one shows. Their answer can reveal where the labels need work.
