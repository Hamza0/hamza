If your French page opens in English, check the destination first. Then confirm the translation exists and whether visitors are seeing an older saved copy.

## Does the link open the right page?

Click the language switcher and watch the address. Then follow the main menu and buttons. A French label can still lead to an English address.

Imagine your Canadian service business uses `/services/` for English and `/fr/services/` for French. The French page reads correctly, but its booking button sends you back to `/services/`. That points to a link problem.

Those addresses are examples. [WPML supports several URL formats](https://wpml.org/documentation/getting-started-guide/language-setup/language-url-options/), including directories and separate domains. Check your site's actual setup before changing links.

## Is the French text available?

If the address is correct, check the content shown at that address. Is the entire page English, or just one paragraph or button?

In TranslatePress, the [translation editor](https://translatepress.com/docs/translation-editor/) lets you inspect individual pieces of text. Editing the original wording can make it a new piece of text that needs translating again.

With WPML, check the translation associated with the page and whether it is available to visitors. The two plugins manage translations differently, so instructions for one may not apply to the other.

## Are visitors seeing an old copy?

A cache keeps saved copies so pages load faster. Compare the same address while signed out, then ask your developer to check the relevant page or language cache.

WPML also [caches its language switcher](https://wpml.org/documentation/customizing-wpml/language-switcher/). An unchanged switcher and an untranslated paragraph need separate checks.

I would finish by following the complete French journey, from the menu to the enquiry form. As with [accessible website design](/blog/accessibility-is-part-of-the-build), the useful check is whether someone can complete their task.
