A website becomes part of a business system when an interaction starts work beyond the page: creating a CRM record, retrieving data, sending a notification or moving a request to another team. Those connections need to be designed and checked alongside the interface.

The distinction helps define a project's scope. It does not mean every website needs accounts, automation or a custom platform.

## Start with the job the website needs to do

A publishing website may need to explain an organisation, present services and help someone find the right contact. Clear content, accessible navigation and a dependable publishing setup can meet that need.

A platform takes on more responsibility when people use it to carry out work. A contact form may assign an enquiry to someone. A resource page may retrieve information from an API. A dashboard may depend on regularly validated data.

The visual design can remain simple in either case. The difference is what must happen around the interaction, including who maintains the information and what the visitor sees when a supporting service is unavailable.

## Follow one interaction from beginning to end

Consider an illustrative website enquiry. The visitor enters details, the receiving system validates them, a service accepts the request and a team follows up.

Each boundary raises a practical question:

- Which information is required, and do the form and receiving system agree on its format?
- Where does the authoritative record live?
- Who owns the next step?
- What can the interface confirm if a connection fails?
- How can someone investigate an enquiry that did not reach its destination?

A form can look complete while sending the wrong fields. A successful page load can conceal an incomplete API response. Following the full path helps identify work that a visual review would miss.

[API timeouts and retries](/blog/apis-are-where-systems-start-talking) are one example of why the visible result and the completed business action need separate checks.

## Make ownership and maintenance clear

A useful integration has an owner. Someone should know who can change its configuration, which service holds the current record and where to look when the connection stops working.

Content needs ownership too. An update that is easy for a developer may be awkward for the person publishing information every week. The editing workflow belongs in the design, alongside the customer-facing pages.

Keep field mappings and important assumptions understandable. If two systems store the same value differently, document how the integration handles that difference. That gives the next person enough context to maintain the connection.

## Explain the information around the tool

My workforce data experience has also made me pay attention to the path into a tool. A chart or planning interface needs context about what its information represents, where it comes from and how someone can use it.

Labels, source information, accessible controls and clear empty states help people interpret the result. These are part of [building data tools for non-technical teams](/blog/building-data-tools-for-non-technical-teams), even when the underlying calculation already works.

## Choose the smallest useful scope

Write down the outcome before adding another integration. If a content site and a dependable form meet the need, keep the build focused. If the task crosses several systems, include those connections, their failure paths and their owners in the project scope.

Before launch, follow the intended user journey with realistic content. Check the handoff, the receiving system and the recovery path, then leave enough information for the team maintaining it.
